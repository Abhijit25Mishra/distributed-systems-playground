## Findings

**FNV-1a alone is unusable for virtual node ids, and the failure is silent.**
This was the session's real finding. FNV-1a's last operation is
`imul(h ^ lastChar, PRIME)`, so two strings sharing a prefix differ by a small
integer multiple of the prime. Virtual node ids are exactly that shape:

```
n1#0  1598427531
n1#1  1581649912   diff -16777619  = -1.00 x FNV prime
n1#2  1631982769   diff  50332857  = +3.00 x FNV prime
n1#3  1615205150   diff -16777619  = -1.00 x FNV prime
```

Ten replicas spanned **5.86%** of the ring where uniform would be ~82%. So all
150 replicas of a node sat in a few tight clumps, virtual nodes bought nothing,
and load skew did not fall as V rose:

| V | plain FNV-1a | FNV + fmix32 | theory 1/sqrt(V) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.5439 | 0.5185 | 1.0000 |
| 50 | 0.5640 | 0.0592 | 0.1414 |
| 200 | 0.1375 | 0.0460 | 0.0707 |
| 500 | 0.1450 | 0.0519 | 0.0447 |

Adding MurmurHash3's `fmix32` finalizer fixed it. After: ten replicas span
91.5% of the ring, and skew tracks theory.

**Keys were never the problem, which is why it hid.** Key bin CV measured
0.0164 against 0.0126 for uniform — fine. `hash` is only weak on short
prefix-sharing inputs, so the key-distribution test passed throughout. The
same function was good enough for `assignModN` (which reads low bits via `%`)
and bad for the ring (which reads high bits as position). One hash, two
different quality requirements.

**The measured headline, which is what the page exists to show:**

```
adding a 5th node to 4, over 100,000 keys
  consistent hashing moved: 19.5%     (theory 20.0%)
  hash % N moved:           80.0%
```

**Node positions do not depend on the seed, and that makes V=1 misleading.**
Caught while picking a default. At V=1 the churn figure reads ~2% for *every*
seed:

```
seeds      1     7    42    99   256   512   777
V=1      1.7   1.8   2.3   1.3   1.2   2.3   2.2
V=120   17.5  16.0  17.7  18.5  18.5  17.5  20.2
```

Not noise. Positions come from `hash("n5#0")`, a fixed value; the seed only
changes the keys. `n5` happens to land just behind its predecessor and inherits
a sliver, so the number is both wrong and stable, which is the worst
combination. The figure now opens at V=120, where the number is representative;
dragging down to 1 still shows the lumpiness.

**Labels collide at high V, and "widest arc" stops meaning anything.** At 150
virtual nodes every arc is a sliver, so the widest one is effectively arbitrary
and two labels landed on top of each other at 12 o'clock. Fixed with an angular
relaxation pass that preserves position when there is room. Needed, not
cosmetic: past three simultaneous series colour cannot carry identity on a
ring, so an unreadable label means an unidentifiable node.

## Session 2: the figure showed the answer but not the mechanism

Committed and pushed everything above first (four commits, `648f3e1`), then
took the feedback that the figure had no play/pause and no way to step through
time, and that it should show a request arriving and being routed to the ring
and then to a virtual node.

That critique was right and it was not about controls. The static figure showed
the *result* of consistent hashing and never the *mechanism*. The mechanism is
two steps that must be seen separately: a key is hashed to a position, which is
a property of the key alone and knows nothing about the cluster; only then is
that position walked clockwise to whichever replica happens to be next. That
split is the entire reason adding a node moves 1/(N+1) of the keys.

Built: `trace.ts` (routing trace and a five-phase clock), `timeline.ts` (cursor
model), `useTimeline.ts` (rAF playback), `transport.tsx` (controls and a step
narration), plus a flight overlay in the renderer. Tests went 35 to 59.

The cursor is one float: integer part selects the request, fraction is flight
progress. Playing advances it, scrubbing sets it, stepping rounds it, so
seeking backwards is exact and free rather than an undo stack.

## Findings

**Choosing the default by the wrong measure, then by the right one.** First
pick was V=16 on the grounds that its mean arc was 5.6 degrees and "plainly
visible". Wrong measure. What matters is the distribution of walks real keys
take, in pixels at the radius the thing is drawn at:

```
 V    walk p25 / median / p75 px    churn (10 seeds)     skew
 4         17 /  48 / 104           31.3% (28.8-32.9)    0.248
 6         12 /  37 /  76           22.0% (19.7-23.3)    0.389
 8          9 /  23 /  47           19.0% (16.4-21.1)    0.331
12          6 /  14 /  30           18.9% (16.9-20.9)    0.231
16          4 /  10 /  20           23.0% (21.5-25.6)    0.217
```

A 10px median walk reads as the key not having moved, which is the one step the
figure exists to show. V=8 doubles it to 23px and happens to have the best
churn of anything tested. Confirmed by screenshot: at V=16 the walk was 2.6
degrees and I could not see it.

**The skew number was mostly noise, and I had asserted it was not.** Measured
against the converged value for this topology (0.217 at V=16, from 200k keys),
the worst error across ten seeds by key count:

```
 600 keys -> 63%     1500 -> 27%     3000 -> 14%
1000 keys -> 33%     2000 -> 17%     5000 ->  8%
```

At the old default of 600 the displayed skew swung 0.148 to 0.355 purely on
which keys were drawn. Raised the default to 2000. Cheap now, because the key
dots are batched into one path per node instead of one fill per key.

**Only 257 of 999 seeds produce a run containing a wrap.** The wrap is the case
`ring.ts` calls "the single most common bug in a consistent hashing
implementation", and at a random seed the figure had a 74% chance of never
showing it. Searched for a seed whose twelve requests include exactly one wrap
with no invisible walks: 444. Request 2 (`doc:1271`) hashes to 357.8 degrees, a
hair before 12 o'clock, and walks 27 degrees clockwise through zero to n2#4.
That is choosing which example to show, not changing what happens.

**The accent and series slot 2 are both orange.** `#e8853f` against `#eb6834`
in dark. A 4px accent trail inside a 10px orange band read as a thin stripe of
n2 rather than as something passing over it, so the live marker looked like a
node. Neither colour is movable, so they are separated by a gap instead: the
trail is stroked twice, a wider pass in the surface colour then the accent on
top. Works regardless of which node the key flies across.

## Session 3: twelve requests, two thousand keys, and no relationship between them

Feedback: "when we are saying there are 2000 keys why are we showing the
animation for 12 requests". Correct, and it is structural rather than cosmetic.

I had built the animated requests as a **separate population** from the key
cloud. The cloud held 2000 keys from `generateKeys`; the requests were twelve
invented names (`cart:9918`) that were not among them. So the figure asserted a
scale and then animated twelve things unrelated to it. Two other symptoms
pointed at the same fault and I had missed both: routed marks were drawn at the
*band* radius while cloud keys sit at 0.86R, so they read as different kinds of
object; and the invented names implied a labelling the cloud did not have.

Fixed by making a request literally one of the keys on screen: `sampleRequests`
draws evenly spaced indices out of the real key array, the label is `key 1251`,
the resolve phase settles the flying dot inward into the cloud while blending
from the accent to the owner's colour, and the panel says "one of 2,000 keys on
the ring. every other key got here the same way." Twelve is now visibly a
sample rather than a separate cast.

## Findings

**Re-derived the default seed, because the old one was chosen for a request
list that no longer exists.** With requests sampled from the key set, which of
them wrap depends on the seed through the keys. Searched all 999 for a seed
with exactly one wrap, no walk under 10px, a median walk over 25px, and
coverage of more than two nodes. **Exactly one seed of 999 satisfied all four:
54.** Its wrap lands on request 4, so autoplay reaches it in about ten seconds
rather than at the end of the run, and the twelve requests between them touch
all four nodes. Churn 21.1% against a theoretical 20.0%, skew 0.371 against
1/sqrt(8) = 0.354.

**On the three.js question.** Read the taste skill and the dataviz skill before
answering. Recommending against, and the reason is accuracy rather than taste:
this figure exists to compare arc sizes and walk lengths, and perspective
foreshortening makes equal arcs subtend unequal angles on screen. Adding depth
would corrupt the exact quantity being measured, which is the 3D pie chart
mistake. The taste skill also scopes three.js to "canvas backgrounds and 3D
scenes" and flags it as large; the dataviz skill treats decoration on a value
scale as a defect. Separately it would need a parallel token path, since WebGL
takes floats and `vizTokens` hands out CSS strings, which is the "eleven
private palettes" failure the rule exists to prevent.

## Session 4: routing all of them, and a slider that reaches the useful range

Feedback, and it was the same objection sharpened: "ITS STILL HAVING 12 i want
it to have the animation for the same number of keys that we are showing". Also
that the key slider should start at 1 rather than 200, top out at 2000, and
favour the 1-10 and 10-20 range "because thats how humans tend to understand".

Correct on all three. Session 3 fixed *which* keys were animated but kept the
count at twelve, so the figure still said 2000 and showed 12. Sampling was
never the fix; routing every key is. `buildRequests` now returns one request
per key, so N keys means N flights and the arithmetic cannot disagree.

The slider is logarithmic. A linear 1-2000 track gives the first ten keys 0.5%
of its length, about two pixels, so the entire range where the mechanism is
followable one key at a time was unreachable. By ratio:

```
   0% -> 1      30% -> 10      60% -> 96      90% -> 935
  10% -> 2      40% -> 21      70% -> 205    100% -> 2000
  20% -> 5      50% -> 45      80% -> 437
```

31% of the travel covers 1-10 keys, 40% covers 1-20. Every integer from 1 to 20
is exactly reachable, which is asserted in a test.

Speeds widened to 0.125x-8x on request. Seven rates as buttons would have cost
~240px of a 512px bar and squeezed the scrubber back to the ~80px that made it
unusable two sessions ago, so it became a select instead: one control wide, and
keyboard and screen-reader complete for free.

## Findings

**One pace cannot serve both ends of the slider.** At the readable 2.4s per
key, eight keys take twenty seconds and two thousand take eighty minutes. So
the pace is derived from the count: full speed while a run fits in about half a
minute, compressing after that, floored so the longest run stays under sixty
seconds. The phases do not vanish as it compresses, they stop being separately
readable, which is honest -- at two thousand keys the interesting object is the
distribution, not any one walk.

**Below half a second the narration had to stop animating.** Five rows
rewriting forty times a second is unreadable and unpleasant. Under
`READABLE_FLIGHT_SECONDS` the panel shows the whole trace at once for whichever
key it caught, held for 220ms. The canvas keeps full frame rate, because a
moving dot at 40Hz is what a moving dot should look like.

**Performance at the top of every slider: 1.47ms per redraw.** Measured in the
browser at 2000 keys, 8 nodes and 200 virtual nodes each, which is 1600
replicas. Two changes bought that: key dots batched into one `Path2D` per node
rather than a fill per key, and computing only the current key's trace instead
of all N up front. Tracing all of them was fine at twelve and would have been
2000 x 1600 arc comparisons on every parameter change.

**Unbalanced at low counts is the lesson, not a defect.** Confirmed with the
human mid-session. At 6 keys the figure reads 33.3% against 83.3%, which is 2
of 6 against 5 of 6, and skew hits 1.000 at 8 keys with two nodes empty. I had
started building a caveat for exactly this and dropped it: dragging the slider
and watching the numbers settle is the thing being taught.
